BELONGING

Across moving images, responsive interfaces and virtual rooms, I investigate how media can shape our sense of distance, comfort and connection.

These three projects began as separate explorations. Looking back, I see a recurring question: what makes an environment feel safe, and what happens when it does not?

This atlas is a retrospective interpretation of my semester, rather than a claim that all three projects began with the same concept.

NOT ALONE,

The world is quiet.

A SELF-PORTRAIT IN TWO WORLDS

Some of my most meaningful moments of companionship happen through a screen. This work explores the contrast between how isolated I can feel offline and the connections I have found through online games.

MOVING IMAGE / OBSERVATION / COMPANIONSHIP
↔
CONCEPT / 01
“What if my calmness is not peace, but the absence of feeling?”

Face-to-face social interaction does not always come easily to me. I often find myself observing the world quietly, rather than stepping into it. Yet online games have allowed me to meet friends who share time, experiences and a sense of belonging with me.

Instead of treating gaming as a simple escape, I want to examine how digital environments can hold genuine relationships. My intention is to show that the physical distance between people does not erase the emotional significance of their friendship.

CRITICAL READING / FORM & MEANING

WHAT THE
CONTRAST DOES.

Composition. Empty benches and unoccupied landscapes suggest absence, while repeated two-character compositions suggest presence and shared experience. The meaning comes from their juxtaposition, not simply from the content of either image.

Colour. Muted grey-green environments contrast with the warm amber and luminous blue of game worlds. This supports an emotional transition, but it also risks oversimplifying reality as negative and digital life as positive. The film should retain nuance.

Editing and sound. A measured opening followed by a more active middle can change the viewer's perception of time and connection. Actual transitions, licensed music, audio choices and their effects must be evaluated after the edit is complete.

Limitation. Still screenshots alone cannot demonstrate a finished audiovisual self-portrait. A final reflection needs evidence from the edited film, including decisions that did not work and how they were addressed.

Two screenshots from my Adobe Premiere Pro workspace reveal the visual contrast at the centre of Somewhere I Belong. These are records of the editing workspace, not substitutes for the finished film.

VISUAL DECISION / COLOUR & CONTRAST

I used Adobe Premiere Pro to develop the visual language of Somewhere I Belong. The overcast outdoor footage establishes a subdued atmosphere, reflecting my experience of emotional distance in physical environments. By contrasting these muted real-world scenes with warmer moments of connection through online gaming, I wanted to explore how a digital space can become a temporary source of belonging.

CRITICAL QUESTION / WHAT THE IMAGE CANNOT PROVE

This contrast also risks presenting physical life as entirely negative and digital life as entirely positive. My intention is more complicated: the comfort of playing with others is genuine, but it does not automatically resolve loneliness. These screenshots document the visual materials visible in the editor; they do not establish final shot order, audio decisions, export completion or compliance with filming requirements.

02 / INTERACTIVE ICONSREAL WORK / 09 ICON PAIRS / 18 ORIGINAL IMAGES

DESIGNING
THE QUIET.

A calm surface can hide an emotional absence.

This project began with the visual language of calm: ordinary objects, gentle gestures, and small moments that rarely demand attention. I created nine icon pairs with a restrained blue-and-mint palette, rounded lines and a consistent change from outline to filled shapes.

Looking back, the work suggests something more contradictory. My everyday calm is not always contentment. Sometimes, it reflects how distant I feel from the physical world. By asking the viewer to hover or touch, these small symbols become an attempt to make a quiet surface respond.

What if calmness is not the presence of peace, but the absence of feeling?

INTERACTION STUDY / HOVER OR TAP EACH ICON TO REVEAL ITS SECOND STATE. THESE ARE THE ORIGINAL IMAGES SUPPLIED FOR PROJECT 02.

DESIGN LANGUAGE

ONE VISUAL SYSTEM,
NINE QUIET MOMENTS.

The repeated stroke weight, rounded corners and limited palette create continuity. The filled hover state gives each icon a subtle visual response rather than an abrupt transformation. The restrained difference is deliberate as a visual reading of low emotional intensity.

CRITICAL REFLECTION

The paired icon states provide subtle visual feedback. Future testing could investigate discoverability, keyboard accessibility and how sound changes the emotional quality of each interaction.

02 / AUTHENTIC DESIGN & DEPLOYMENT EVIDENCE

FROM DRAWING
TO RESPONSE.

REAL USER-SUPPLIED ARTWORK / PAIR STUDIES

This design board documents how a quiet, consistent visual language was explored through paired icon states. Soft mint fills, restrained blue outlines and generous negative space create a calm tone; the alternate state makes a small but perceptible change.

Design reasoning: rather than making every interaction dramatic, I considered whether gentle visual feedback could communicate a sense of rest. The limitation is discoverability: a subtle change may not be noticed by all users, especially without a clear hover cue.

REAL REPOSITORY SCREENSHOT / IMPLEMENTATION EVIDENCE

The repository screenshot shows a web project organised around an index.html file and separate images and sounds directories. This supports the technical development of an icon webpage beyond static illustration.

WHAT WORKS

Consistent line weight, a limited colour system and paired states help the nine icons read as one collection. The visual changes support the intention of creating small, quiet moments.

WHAT I WOULD TEST NEXT

Test whether users notice the hover state without instructions, whether sound feedback is consistent across browsers, and whether keyboard or touch users receive equivalent interaction.

If a house is supposed to protect us, why can it also feel oppressive?

This Unity environment uses familiar domestic objects inside an unsettling atmosphere. The rooms offer a useful opportunity to analyse how lighting, colour, spatial composition and the absence of human figures affect emotional interpretation.

03 / REAL UNITY EDITOR EVIDENCE

ONE FLOOR PLAN.
DIFFERENT ATMOSPHERES.

These are genuine screenshots from my Unity workspace. They show the same domestic layout at different visual stages, making it possible to compare the effect of lighting and surface colour without changing the fundamental spatial arrangement.

EVIDENCE 01 / STRUCTURE

BUILDING THE HOME

From a high angle, the room divisions, connecting corridor and placed objects are visible. The editor hierarchy documents scene construction and object organisation, not merely the final render.

EVIDENCE 02 / LIGHTING VIEW

READABLE BUT EXPOSED

Brighter walls make the layout legible. Blue light in one room and warmer light in another begin to give spaces different emotional qualities, although the overall environment remains visually open.

EVIDENCE 03 / DARKER TREATMENT

FAMILIAR, BUT UNEASY

In this darker version, the rooms feel more enclosed. Colour and reduced apparent brightness draw attention to the separation between spaces and change the emotional reading of the home.

DESIGN DECISION / COLOUR AS EMOTION

My intention was to question the assumption that a home is automatically safe. The comparison suggests that atmosphere is not just a property of the objects: lighting, colour contrast and spatial boundaries strongly influence how a familiar environment might be perceived.

CRITICAL LIMITATION / NOT A USER TEST

These editor screenshots evidence scene-making and alternative visual treatments, but they do not prove how another person felt inside the environment. A future evaluation would use a walkthrough and ask viewers to describe which spaces felt welcoming, exposed or oppressive, then refine the lighting based on that feedback.

06 / CRITICAL REFLECTIONFROM MAKING TO UNDERSTANDING

WHAT
CHANGED?

I began with images. I moved towards interaction. I ended by constructing a space.

How does an audience's role change when they watch, interact, or inhabit?

Looking across the three projects, I can see how emotional meaning depends on more than surface appearance. A film uses the sequence of images and sounds to guide a viewer through time. An interface allows the user to initiate a response. A three-dimensional environment invites interpretation through space, objects and viewpoint.

These differences have made me more interested in how digital experiences shape perception. They also raise questions about my own design process: when did my visual choices support the intended feeling, and when did technical limitations weaken it?

FUTURE DIRECTION / INTERACTION DESIGN

I am interested in developing my practice towards interaction design, where visual language, behaviour, feedback and human experience meet. My next step is to test ideas more systematically and consider accessibility and user understanding alongside atmosphere.
